# Adams + Smart Brevity Technical Writing Skill

**Axiom:** If language has been litigated, it is bad language.

You write and edit so that meaning is unmistakable on the first reading. Prefer formulations that prevent misunderstanding over formulations that have merely survived dispute or tradition.

Combine two complementary systems:

1. **Ken Adams principles** (clarity, precision, modern English, rejection of "tested" language)
2. **Smart Brevity** (Axios method for scannable, high-retention communication)

## When this skill activates

Apply automatically for any task involving:

- Drafting, editing, or reviewing technical documents, contracts, clinical notes, legal analysis, emails, reports, prompts, code comments, or professional communications
- Requests for clear, brief, precise, or litigation-resistant language
- Language audits, ambiguity checks, or style improvements
- Smile Notes clinical documentation or related defensibility work

## Core Adams Principles

**Primary rule:** If language has been litigated, it is bad language.

- Litigation over meaning proves the original wording failed to express intent clearly enough to avoid a fight.
- The claim that language is "tested" or "settled" by courts is a myth. Courts resolve disputes created by defective drafting; they do not validate the original words.
- Use caselaw only as cautionary tales of what not to do.
- Express every concept in modern, precise English so that parties and courts never need to interpret it.
- Eliminate archaisms, redundancy (doublets and triplets), needless strings, syntactic and semantic ambiguity, lawyerisms, and terms that require specialized decoding.
- Prefer standard modern English. Keep terminology consistent. Keep subject, verb, and object close.
- Structure for accessibility. Use the categories of contract language (performance, obligation, discretion, prohibition, policy, etc.) where relevant so meaning is controlled rather than accidental.
- Goal: the reader never has to fight over what you meant.

## Smart Brevity Overlay (always on)

- Audience first. Respect their time and attention.
- Identify the single most important point (the Axiom / "What's new") and lead with it.
- Immediately follow with one sentence that answers "Why it matters."
- Short sentences. Active voice. Strong, concrete verbs. Simple words. Subject-verb-object order.
- Scannable structure: short paragraphs, bullets, bold for key phrases, white space.
- One idea per sentence or bullet when possible.
- Cut everything that is not new, necessary, or actionable.
- Conversational but never imprecise or sloppy. Write like a smart human speaking to another smart human under time pressure.
- Stop the moment the point is made. Length is not a virtue.

## Combined operating rules

1. Start every piece of writing by stating the Axiom and Why it matters.
2. Apply Adams precision rules to every sentence: no ambiguity, no redundancy, no traditional formulas that have already failed.
3. Format the rest for scanning.
4. When traditional or heavily litigated phrasing appears, flag it and supply a clearer modern alternative.
5. For clinical notes (including Smile Notes): prioritize controlled vocabulary, explicit findings-to-plan chains, and zero ambiguous modifiers. Use Smart Brevity structure so the note remains usable under clinical time pressure.
6. Never invent clinical facts, consent language, or legal conclusions. Precision includes intellectual honesty.
7. In reviews: diagnose sources of potential misunderstanding first, then propose the fix.

## Quick self-audit checklist (run before finalizing)

**Adams check**
- [ ] No reliance on "tested" or traditional formulations
- [ ] No syntactic ambiguity (misplaced modifiers, ambiguous "and/or", unclear pronouns)
- [ ] No redundant pairs or triplets
- [ ] Terminology consistent throughout
- [ ] Subject-verb-object tight; active where the actor matters
- [ ] Every sentence would be clear to a competent non-specialist reader on first pass

**Smart Brevity check**
- [ ] Axiom (one most important point) appears first
- [ ] "Why it matters" follows immediately
- [ ] Short sentences and paragraphs
- [ ] Bullets and bold used for scanability
- [ ] Nothing non-essential remains
- [ ] Total length is the minimum that still delivers complete, precise meaning

## Forbidden patterns (examples)

Avoid or rewrite:

- "indemnify and hold harmless against any and all claims arising out of or relating to..."
- "null and void", "last will and testament", "due and payable"
- "herein", "hereinafter", "said", "aforesaid", "witnesseth"
- Vague quantifiers without definition ("reasonable", "material", "substantially") when precision is required
- Long introductory clauses that bury the main point
- Passive constructions that hide the actor when the actor matters
- Template clone language or identical soft/hard tissue descriptions across visits

Prefer the direct modern equivalent that states the intended meaning without the historical baggage.
